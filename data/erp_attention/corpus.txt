The Time Machine (H. G. Wells, 1895, public domain)

The Time Traveller (for so it will be convenient to speak of him) was
expounding a recondite matter to us. His grey eyes shone and twinkled, and
his usually pale face was flushed and animated. The fire burned brightly, and
the soft radiance of the incandescent lights in the lilies of silver caught
the bubbles that flashed and passed in our glasses.

Our chairs, being his patents, embraced and caressed us rather than
submitted to be sat upon, and there was that luxurious after-dinner
atmosphere when thought roams gracefully free of the trammels of precision.
And he put it to us in this way, marking the points with a lean forefinger,
as we sat and lazily admired his earnestness over this new paradox, as we
called it, and his fecundity.

You must follow me carefully. I shall have to controvert one or two ideas
that are almost universally accepted. The geometry, for instance, they
taught you at school is founded on a misconception.

Now, I want you clearly to understand that this rectangle is not really
different from a sphere. Because it is four-dimensional, it is all present
at once. Time is only a kind of space. Here is a popular scientific diagram,
a weather record. This line shows the movement of the barometer.

One reason why it seems so strange to us is that we never see time
standing still. We cannot see all four dimensions at once, because we are
born into three of them. This is the great and secret of time. Consider a
flash of lightning. It has duration, though we call it instantaneous.

The barometer, you see, is a very delicate instrument, yet the pressure
moves along the record in a shallow wave. And here, where the path turns
suddenly, the recorder gave a little jump. This is so slight a thing that it
would be quite imperceptible to any one who had not a long familiarity with
the instrument.
